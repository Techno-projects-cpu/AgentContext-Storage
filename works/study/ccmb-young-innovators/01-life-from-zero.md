# Life from zero: a curious student's biology toolkit 🧬

These are foundations for independent preparation, not a published CCMB syllabus. Analogies are models, not perfect descriptions.

## 1. How can tiny things make a living person?

A **cell (కణం)** is the basic structural and functional unit of life. Many living organisms consist of one cell; humans and plants have many. Cells need materials and energy and interact with their surroundings. An **organelle** is a specialised structure within a cell. Think of a cell as a busy town, but remember: a real cell is not consciously governed like a town.

| Structure | Main job | Helpful distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Cell membrane (కణ త్వచం) | Selectively regulates movement into and out of the cell | Plant **and** animal cells have membranes. |
| Cytoplasm (కణద్రవ్యం) | Material within the cell where many processes occur | It is not just empty water. |
| Nucleus (కేంద్రకం) | Holds most DNA in plant and animal cells | Bacteria lack a membrane-bound nucleus, but have DNA. |
| Mitochondria | Help produce usable cellular energy through respiration | Plant cells have mitochondria too. |
| Ribosomes | Make proteins | Found in bacterial cells as well. |
| Cell wall (కణ గోడ) | Gives support and protection | Plant walls contain cellulose; bacterial walls have different composition. |
| Chloroplast | Site of photosynthesis in plant cells that contain it | Not every plant cell has chloroplasts; roots generally don't. |

**Order:** cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. For example, muscle cells form muscle tissue, which forms part of organs such as the heart.

**Check:** Does an animal cell have a cell membrane? **Yes.** A cell wall? **No.** Does a plant cell respire? **Yes**, day and night.

## 2. A cell's information: DNA → RNA → protein

**DNA (డీఎన్‌ఏ)** is a molecule that stores biological information. It is made of smaller building blocks, including the bases A, T, C and G. A **gene (జన్యువు)** is a segment of DNA containing information for a functional product, often a protein. Humans inherit DNA from both biological parents.

In the usual information pathway, DNA information is copied into **RNA** (transcription); ribosomes use the information in messenger RNA to assemble a **protein (మాంసకృత్తి)** (translation). This simplified pathway is often called DNA → RNA → protein. Not every gene makes a protein; some produce functional RNA.

Proteins can serve as structural materials, transporters, signals or **enzymes**. A gene change (**mutation**) may alter a product, have no noticeable effect, or have other effects depending on context. Environment can also influence traits. DNA is not a simple one-gene guarantee for every characteristic.

**Analogy:** DNA is like a library of instructions, RNA like a working copy, and a ribosome like a workshop. But cells don't read English or work exactly like machines.

**Cloning a gene** broadly means making copies of a DNA segment, often using specialised tools and cells. It is not the same as cloning an entire person. CCMB's earlier supervised student activity is described in its [press note](https://ccmb.res.in/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/pressnote_05_01_2026.pdf); no lab procedures are required here.

## 3. Microbes: useful, harmful or neither?

**Microorganisms (సూక్ష్మజీవులు)** include bacteria, many fungi and many other microscopic organisms. Viruses are tiny infectious agents; whether they count as living is debated. They use a host cell's machinery to reproduce.

- **Bacteria:** single-celled organisms without a membrane-bound nucleus. Some help make curd or contribute to nutrient cycles; some cause disease.
- **Fungi:** include yeasts and moulds. Yeast fermentation produces carbon dioxide that can make bread dough rise.
- **Viruses:** examples include influenza virus. Antibiotics target bacterial structures or processes; they **do not treat influenza itself**. Some patients may also have bacterial infections, which require a clinician's judgement.
- **Vaccines** train the immune system to recognise a threat; **antiserum** provides ready-made antibodies, giving passive protection. Medical decisions belong to qualified professionals.

**Antimicrobial resistance:** microbes can evolve or acquire ways to survive medicines that would otherwise work. It is the microorganisms—not a person's body—that become resistant. [WHO explanation](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/antimicrobial-resistance).

**Trap:** “All bacteria are harmful” is false. “Antibiotics cure all infections” is also false.

## 4. Enzymes and chemistry

An **enzyme (ఎంజైమ్)** is a biological catalyst: it increases the rate of a particular reaction and is not consumed overall. Most enzymes are proteins. They help reactions happen through a lower-activation-energy pathway; they do not create energy or change the final equilibrium by themselves.

Enzyme activity can depend on temperature, pH and concentrations. For many enzymes, raising temperature initially speeds reaction, but excessive heat can disrupt structure (**denaturation**) and reduce activity. Different enzymes have different conditions; avoid claiming that all enzymes have one fixed optimum.

**pH:** lower pH means greater hydrogen-ion activity. At 25°C, pure neutral water has pH 7; acidic solutions are below 7, basic ones above 7. A one-unit change is a tenfold change in hydrogen-ion activity: pH 4 has ten times the activity of pH 5. For dilute school examples this is often approximated using hydrogen-ion concentration.

**Physical vs chemical change:** melting ice changes state, but it remains water. Rusting iron forms new substances. A colour change can be a clue but is not by itself absolute proof of a chemical reaction.

## 5. Getting substances and energy where needed

- **Diffusion (వ్యాప్తి):** net movement of particles from a region of higher concentration to a lower concentration due to random motion. Oxygen can diffuse across lung surfaces.
- **Osmosis (ద్రవాభిసరణం):** net movement of water through a selectively permeable membrane, typically from a more dilute solution toward a more concentrated one. Dry raisins swelling in water are a familiar example.
- **Photosynthesis (కిరణజన్య సంయోగక్రియ):** plants use light energy to produce sugars from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen. The released oxygen comes from water. The process is more complex than one short equation.
- **Cellular respiration (కణ శ్వాసక్రియ):** cells transfer energy from food into usable forms. Plants and animals respire. Oxygen is used in aerobic respiration, but not every organism or pathway needs oxygen.
- **Xylem** mainly transports water and minerals; **phloem** transports sugars and other organic substances. Stomata help gas exchange.

**Trap:** Photosynthesis and respiration are not identical. Plants respire even in darkness, though photosynthesis requires light.

## 6. Body systems and immunity

The lungs exchange gases; the circulatory system transports them. Red blood cells carry oxygen mainly using haemoglobin. Arteries carry blood **away** from the heart and veins carry blood **towards** it. The pulmonary artery is an important example carrying oxygen-poor blood away from the heart to the lungs.

The immune system helps defend against threats; it is not infallible. Healthy decisions require reliable medical advice, not viral messages.

## 7. Five quick self-checks

1. Why can antibiotics target bacteria but not the influenza virus itself? **They target bacterial structures or processes that the virus does not have.**
2. Can a plant cell contain both chloroplasts and mitochondria? **Yes**, in cells with chloroplasts.
3. What is the basic DNA-to-protein pathway? **DNA → RNA → protein**, with exceptions and additional regulation.
4. Why might an enzyme slow down in extreme heat? **Its structure can be disrupted.**
5. A raisin swells in water. Which movement is central? **Net water entry by osmosis.**
