# Think like a scientist: experiments, data and evidence 🔍

[Start page](README.md) · [Biology foundations](01-life-from-zero.md) · [Mock B](05-mock-research.md)

Science is not just a collection of answers. It is a way to test ideas, consider alternatives and revise conclusions. These examples are thought exercises, not instructions to perform lab work.

## 1. From question to conclusion

Imagine asking: **Does light affect the growth of a particular kind of seedling over 10 days?**

- **Hypothesis (పరికల్పన):** under the stated conditions, seedlings given more light will grow taller over 10 days. It is testable; it is not a fact until evidence supports it.
- **Independent variable (స్వతంత్ర చరరాశి):** light exposure, deliberately varied between groups.
- **Dependent variable (ఆధారిత చరరాశి):** measured growth, for example height change in centimetres.
- **Controlled variables:** similar species, starting age, water, soil, temperature and measurement time.
- **Control/comparison:** a similar group with the baseline light condition. There is not always a single universal control; it depends on the question.
- **Replicates:** several seedlings in each group; this helps distinguish a general pattern from variation in one plant.

If the bright group receives more water too, **light and water are confounded**: either, both or another factor could explain a difference. Try to vary one main factor at a time when possible. Randomly assigning comparable seedlings to groups reduces systematic selection bias; it does not guarantee identical groups.

## 2. Controls are not magic

Suppose a new solution is claimed to help seeds germinate. A similar group given only the usual water could be a **negative/baseline control**. A **positive control**, when available, receives a condition known to produce the effect and helps check whether the test can detect it. Avoid treating a baseline as proof that every other condition is identical.

A result can be unexpected because the original idea was wrong, the measurement was noisy, conditions differed, the sample was small, or equipment failed. Repeat and investigate rather than hide data.

## 3. Observation ≠ explanation

- **Observation:** 8 of 10 seedlings in group A grew at least 1 cm; 5 of 10 in group B did.
- **Interpretation:** A had more seedlings meeting that threshold in this sample.
- **Further claim:** the treatment causes growth in every plant. **Not established** by this small observation alone.

Be specific about what was measured and the population to which a conclusion might apply. A study with more samples and careful controls can be more informative; bigger alone does not fix a biased test.

## 4. Data and friendly maths

### Average

Three seedlings grow **2, 3 and 4 cm**. Average = (2 + 3 + 4)/3 = **3 cm**. An average is useful, but it can hide variation: 0, 3 and 6 cm also average 3.

### Percentage vs percentage increase

Suppose 8 out of 20 seeds germinate: 8/20 × 100 = **40%**. If a count rises from 20 to 30, the increase is 10/20 × 100 = **50%**. The final count is **150% of** the original; that's a different statement.

### Ratio and scale

A scale bar says 1 cm on the drawing represents 10 micrometres (µm). A feature drawn 3 cm long corresponds to **30 µm**, provided the stated scale applies to the displayed image. If the image is resized without updating its scale bar, a printed magnification number may mislead.

### Graphs

Read title, axes, units and time before drawing conclusions. An upward graph shows the measured quantity increases; the **slope** on a distance–time graph gives speed for a straight segment. A graph ending at day 5 cannot establish exactly what happened on day 6.

### Probability (intro)

A fair coin's chance of heads is 1/2. Two independent heads in a row have chance 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Independence must not be assumed automatically for biological events.

## 5. Common research traps

1. **Correlation vs causation:** ice-cream sales and swimming can rise together in hot weather; the link alone doesn't show ice cream causes swimming.
2. **No comparison:** one plant grew after fertiliser does not prove it grew *because* of fertiliser.
3. **Small sample:** one plant can be unusual; use comparable replicates.
4. **Cherry-picking:** reporting only trials that support a favourite theory distorts evidence.
5. **Measurement bias:** knowing a group can influence subjective readings; blinded measurement can help where practical.
6. **Unclear units:** 5 mm and 5 cm differ tenfold. Record units and methods.
7. **Absolute claims:** “one study proves this works for everyone” goes beyond most data.
8. **Mixing up final amount and increase:** 40 is 200% of 20, but the increase is 100%.

## 6. What to say when you do not know

A good response: “My best idea is X because of Y. To check it, I would compare A and B, keep these conditions similar, measure Z and consider other explanations.” Curiosity and an honest limitation are more scientific than confident guessing.

## 7. Three thought challenges

**A.** A student gives Plant A fertiliser and more water than Plant B. A grows taller. Can we isolate the fertiliser's effect? **No.** Water differs too; use comparable groups with the same water, several plants and fertiliser as the main difference.

**B.** Five plants each grow 2 cm; another five grow 1, 1, 2, 3 and 3 cm. Do both groups have the same mean? **Yes, 2 cm**, but their variation differs.

**C.** An experiment finds no effect in one short trial. Does that prove the treatment can never work under any conditions? **No.** The measurement, sample, duration and conditions limit the conclusion.

**Safety:** Do not culture microorganisms or experiment with bodily samples, chemicals or electricity at home. Work only under trained supervision in appropriate facilities.
