# Health, food and evidence: how do we know? 🧬

Educational material only; a qualified health professional should diagnose illness and advise treatment.

## Defence and transmission

1. **Immune system:** cells, tissues and molecules that help defend the body. Skin and mucous membranes are among physical barriers; immune cells also respond to threats.
2. **Antigen:** a substance or molecular feature recognised by components of the immune system. **Antibody (ప్రతిరక్షకం):** a protein made by certain immune cells that can bind a specific target.
3. **B cells** can develop into antibody-producing plasma cells. **T cells** have several roles, including helping coordinate immune responses or destroying infected cells.
4. **Immune memory:** after some infections or vaccinations, memory cells can help the body respond more quickly to a later encounter. Protection varies by disease and vaccine.
5. **Vaccination:** introduces an antigen or instructions for producing one in a controlled way to train immune responses. Vaccines are not identical to antibiotics.
6. **Antimicrobial resistance:** microbes change in ways that make treatments less effective. The microbe becomes resistant, not the person's body. Resistance may spread and can affect bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites.
7. **Antibiotic resistance:** specifically concerns bacteria and antibiotics. Antibiotics do not treat the viruses that cause common colds; follow prescribed medical advice and never share medicines.
8. **Zoonosis (జంతువుల నుంచి మనుషులకు వ్యాపించే వ్యాధి):** an infectious disease that can pass between animals and people. Rabies is one example; routes differ among diseases.
9. **Rabies exposure:** a bite or scratch from a potentially rabid animal needs prompt washing and urgent medical advice; timely treatment can prevent disease. Do not wait for symptoms.
10. **Incubation period:** time between exposure to infection and symptom onset. It differs from the time a person may be infectious, and both vary by disease and person.
11. **Vector vs pathogen:** an infected mosquito may transmit a pathogen; the pathogen is the disease-causing agent. Malaria is caused by *Plasmodium* parasites.
12. **Outbreak vs endemic:** an outbreak is an increase beyond what is expected in a time/place; endemic describes regular presence at an expected level. Neither word automatically describes severity.

## Food, labels and care

13. **Food contamination:** biological, chemical or physical material may make food unsafe. Smell and appearance alone cannot detect every hazard.
14. **Cross-contamination:** microbes can move from raw food or unclean utensils to ready-to-eat food. Separate raw and cooked items and clean hands and surfaces.
15. **Cold storage:** refrigeration slows growth of many microbes but does not kill all pathogens or make unsafe food safe. Follow food-specific storage instructions.
16. **Pasteurization:** controlled heating reduces harmful microbes in foods such as milk; it is not the same as sterilization and does not mean the food keeps forever.
17. **Preservatives:** substances or methods that help slow spoilage. Safety depends on the substance, amount and regulation; “contains a preservative” alone does not establish danger.
18. **Food label:** read serving size, ingredients, allergy information, storage instructions and date wording. Nutrient amounts may be per serving or per 100 g; compare equal amounts.
19. **Dehydration:** loss of water and salts can be serious. Oral rehydration solution can help replace them; follow qualified health guidance, especially for children or severe symptoms.
20. **Iron-deficiency anaemia** is one cause of anaemia, not the only one. A diagnosis needs assessment; supplements are not a substitute for appropriate medical care.

## Testing a treatment

21. **Clinical trial:** a study involving people that tests a health intervention under a plan with ethical oversight. Consent and participant safety are essential.
22. **Comparison group:** helps researchers estimate what would have happened without the tested intervention. The appropriate comparison depends on the question and ethics.
23. **Random allocation:** assigning participants by chance can help make groups more comparable, though small samples can still differ.
24. **Blinding:** keeping some participants and/or researchers unaware of group assignment can reduce certain biases. A **placebo** is an inactive comparator when appropriate; it must not replace needed effective care unethically.
25. **Sample size and uncertainty:** a small trial can miss rare harms or yield an imprecise result. Larger or repeated studies can improve evidence but do not eliminate every source of error.
26. **Relative vs absolute change:** if a risk falls from 4 in 100 to 2 in 100, the absolute reduction is 2 percentage points; relative reduction is 50%. Both need a timeframe and comparable groups to be meaningful.
27. A testimonial can suggest a question but cannot by itself establish that a treatment caused improvement. Compare evidence and speak to a health professional.
