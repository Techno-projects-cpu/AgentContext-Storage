# The two questions we should have studied properly 🧪

## 1. The mandal paper: alum on a bleeding wound

**What the paper actually asks:** [Mandal paper page 1](../../completed/paper-workout/original-photos/mandal-page-1.jpg), question 1, describes a minor bleeding injury and potash alum, printed as `K₂SO₄·Al₂(SO₄)₃·24H₂O`. Option **B** describes Al³⁺ destabilising blood colloids and promoting coagulation; that is the apparent **intended** colloid-chemistry answer, not an official key.

### What is alum?

- **Potash alum / potassium alum (పటిక):** `KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O`. The paper's `K₂SO₄·Al₂(SO₄)₃·24H₂O` contains **twice** the formula-unit quantities: both represent the same 1:1 potassium-to-aluminium ratio and 12 waters per `KAl(SO₄)₂` unit.
- A formula unit has **K = potassium, Al = aluminium, S = sulfur, O = oxygen, H = hydrogen**. `SO₄²⁻` is the sulfate ion; `12H₂O` is **water of crystallisation**, not 12 glasses of loose water being pumped into a wound!
- In water, potassium alum dissolves into hydrated ions, commonly simplified as:

  `KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O(s) → K⁺(aq) + Al³⁺(aq) + 2 SO₄²⁻(aq) + 12 H₂O(l)`

  In solution Al³⁺ interacts with water and forms several species depending on pH and conditions. The expression is a simplified ion balance, not a complete description of blood.

### Why might it help a tiny shaving nick?

Aluminium salts have **astringent/styptic** properties: they can interact with and precipitate proteins at a surface, tighten superficial tissue and help reduce minor bleeding. The exam frames this as **positively charged Al³⁺ reducing repulsion between negatively charged colloidal particles**, helping aggregation. This is a useful model of colloid destabilisation, but **normal blood clotting also involves platelets, clotting proteins, vessel responses and other processes**. It would be misleading to claim all bleeding stops solely because Al³⁺ neutralises every particle.

**For real injuries:** this is not an instruction to put lab alum on a wound. Use clean dressing and direct pressure; get urgent help for severe or persistent bleeding. [Red Cross first aid](https://www.redcross.org/take-a-class/resources/learn-first-aid/bleeding-life-threatening-external).

### Different question: muddy water

“Alum” can also mean **aluminium sulfate**, `Al₂(SO₄)₃` (often hydrated), used in treatment plants; it is **not identical in formula** to potash alum. In suitable water conditions, aluminium species can reduce repulsion among suspended particles, and aluminium hydroxide-containing **flocs** can trap particles. Flocs can settle and be filtered. This is a **water-clarification mechanism**, not a complete model of blood clotting. Appropriate dose, pH and follow-up disinfection depend on the water and treatment process. **Clear water is not automatically safe to drink.** [Australian drinking-water guidelines](https://guidelines.nhmrc.gov.au/australian-drinking-water-guidelines/part-5/treatment-chemicals/aluminium-sulfate-alum).

## 2. The school paper: “blood” from a cut lemon

**The actual printed wording matters.** [School paper page 1](../../completed/paper-workout/original-photos/school-page-1.jpg), Q1, describes a fake godman, a cut lemon and blood-red liquid. Its option B refers to **ferric chloride `FeCl₃` and methyl orange OR potassium thiocyanate**. The exact preparation and the intended role of each substance are not fully specified. Earlier our worked guide treated it as only the iron–thiocyanate reaction; that was **too narrow**. There are at least two distinct ways a red colour could be produced:

### Route A — methyl orange + acid (indicator)

1. Lemon juice contains **citric acid**, `C₆H₈O₇`; aqueous acids increase the availability of H⁺ (more accurately, hydronium ions in water).
2. **Methyl orange** is an acid–base indicator (సూచిక). The common sodium salt has formula `C₁₄H₁₄N₃NaO₃S`. It is not iron dioxide.
3. At sufficiently low pH it is mainly in a **red protonated form**; above its transition range it is mainly yellow. Approximate transition: **pH 3.1–4.4**; actual appearance depends on pH and concentration. Lemon juice can be acidic enough to give red.
4. At particle level, acid changes the proportion of indicator molecules in protonated vs unprotonated forms. Their electron arrangements absorb different wavelengths of light, so we see different colours. Schematic: `HIn (red) ⇌ H⁺ + In⁻ (yellow)`; `In` represents the indicator, not a chemical element.
5. **No FeCl₃ is required for this indicator mechanism.** A school-level account explicitly describes methyl orange/hibiscus and lemon juice; [AP Class 7 reference](https://apboardsolutions.in/ap-board-7th-class-science-important-questions-chapter-2/). A weakly acidic solution above the transition need not look red: “acid always means red methyl orange” is too crude.

### Route B — iron(III) + thiocyanate (red complex)

1. **Ferric chloride** = iron(III) chloride, `FeCl₃`, providing iron(III) species (`Fe³⁺`) in solution. It is **not iron dioxide**; `Fe₂O₃` is iron(III) oxide, another substance.
2. **Potassium thiocyanate** `KSCN` supplies `SCN⁻`; ammonium thiocyanate is another possible source of thiocyanate in a different demonstration.
3. A simplified equilibrium is `Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ ⇌ [FeSCN]²⁺`. The reddish iron–thiocyanate complex absorbs visible light differently from the separate reactants. Other complexes may exist depending on conditions. [Harvard demonstration explanation](https://sciencedemonstrations.fas.harvard.edu/presentations/equilibrium-iron-iii-thiocyanate).
4. That red liquid **is not blood**. Lemon juice itself is not known to supply the thiocyanate required for this mechanism; if the ingredients and where they were placed are not specified, don't invent them.

**Do the routes require one another? No.** Acid + methyl orange is an indicator colour change; iron(III) + thiocyanate is complex formation. A staged effect can have other explanations, such as hidden dye. Appearance alone cannot reveal which route occurred. Never perform these chemical tricks on skin or food, and never consume treated fruit.

## Quick exam discrimination

| Clue | Reasonable answer | What it does NOT establish |
|---|---|---|
| Paper's potash alum formula | Potassium aluminium sulfate hydrate | That crystal water is pumped into vessels |
| Al³⁺ and colloidal particles | Charge interactions can promote aggregation | Full medical mechanism of clotting |
| Methyl orange + sufficiently acidic lemon | Indicator's red form | Iron(III)–thiocyanate necessarily present |
| Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ | Red iron–thiocyanate complex | Real blood or supernatural event |
| Red colour alone | Something absorbs light differently | Which of several mechanisms occurred |

A useful test question is “**what else would we need to know?**” With no independent evidence of the reagents, the actual substance used in a performance cannot be identified from colour alone.
