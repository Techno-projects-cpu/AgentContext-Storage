# Everyday science: from your kitchen to the street 🍋

Each card goes from **observation → mechanism → useful limit**. This is not a guide to chemical experimentation, food testing or medical treatment.

## Food and cooking

1. **Curd from milk.** Bacteria ferment lactose (milk sugar), producing lactic acid. Lower pH changes interactions among milk proteins, which form a gel. Fermentation is not simply “milk becoming rotten”; hygiene, culture and temperature matter.
2. **Lemon in milk.** Acid can make milk proteins aggregate into curds; the remaining liquid is whey. Adding acid and bacterial fermentation can both lower pH, but they are **different processes**.
3. **Baking soda:** sodium hydrogen carbonate `NaHCO₃`. With a suitable acid it can release `CO₂`; bubbles in a batter can expand and help it rise. Heating also decomposes it. Amounts and other ingredients matter.
4. **Baking powder vs baking soda.** Baking powder typically contains a bicarbonate and acid ingredients (and often a filler). They are not interchangeable in every recipe; carbon dioxide is the gas doing much of the lifting.
5. **Yeast in dough.** Yeast converts sugars through fermentation; `CO₂` bubbles trapped in dough help it rise. The gas is not “air created by the yeast”; temperature, time and dough structure affect results.
6. **Idli batter.** A community of microorganisms can ferment the batter, producing acids and gas. Exact organisms, proportions and fermentation results vary with ingredients and conditions; a sour smell is not proof of safety.
7. **Cooking an egg.** Heat changes the shapes and interactions of proteins; they aggregate into a firmer network. “Protein destroyed” is an oversimplification; many nutrients remain, although properties change.
8. **Brown cut apple.** Enzymatic oxidation involving plant compounds and oxygen contributes to browning. Lemon juice can slow it partly by changing pH and through antioxidant action; results vary. Browning alone does not tell you all you need to know about safety.
9. **Salt and a raw mango.** Water can move across membranes in response to differences in water potential; salt can draw water from some plant tissues. The full pickle-preservation effect also depends on salt concentration, acidity, hygiene and storage.
10. **Jaggery/sugar dissolving.** Water molecules interact with sugar molecules and separate them into solution; the sugar is dispersed, not destroyed. Salt dissolves as ions, whereas sugar remains mostly as molecules.
11. **Milk and Tyndall effect.** Milk is a complex colloidal mixture with small dispersed droplets and particles that scatter light. It is not a simple true solution of sugar in water.
12. **Why pressure cooking can be faster.** Increased pressure raises the boiling point of water, allowing a higher cooking temperature. It is not because pressure “forces heat inside”. Use the cooker and valve only according to its instructions.
13. **Why a cooker whistles.** Pressure can lift or move the regulator, releasing steam. The exact mechanism varies by design. Never block a safety valve.
14. **Cooking oil and water.** They usually form separate layers because their molecules interact differently. Soap can help disperse oil as droplets, but shaking alone may give only a temporary mixture.

## Washing, cleaning and materials

15. **Soap and grease.** A soap molecule has a water-attracted end and an oil-attracted hydrocarbon tail. In water, aggregates called **micelles** can surround oily material so it can be rinsed away. Agitation and rinsing also matter.
16. **Hard water and soap.** Dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ can form poorly soluble salts with soap, producing scum and reducing lather. Some detergents work better than ordinary soap in hard water; not all are identical.
17. **Why soap helps remove germs.** Handwashing lifts oil, dirt and many microbes off skin; soap can also disrupt lipid envelopes of some viruses. Rubbing and rinsing matter. It does not destroy every pathogen instantly.
18. **Bleach is not soap.** Many household bleaches are oxidising agents; a common active ingredient is sodium hypochlorite, `NaOCl`. **Never mix bleach with acids, toilet cleaner or ammonia**: hazardous gases can result. Follow product directions.
19. **Turmeric on a soap stain.** Turmeric contains curcumin, which can turn reddish-brown in a sufficiently basic medium. Turmeric can act as an indicator; the exact colour depends on the mixture and concentration.
20. **Rusting.** Iron reacts in the presence of oxygen and moisture to form hydrated iron oxides. Salt water can speed corrosion by increasing electrical conductivity; rust is not simply a layer of dirt.
21. **Galvanising.** A zinc coating helps shield iron/steel and can offer sacrificial protection where a small scratch exposes steel. Protection has limits and depends on conditions.
22. **Stainless steel.** Chromium in the alloy helps form a thin protective oxide film. “Stainless” means corrosion-resistant, not incapable of ever rusting.
23. **Limescale in a kettle.** Some dissolved calcium compounds can form calcium carbonate deposits when water is heated. Not all hard water is softened by boiling; kettle scale does not identify every dissolved substance.
24. **Alum and muddy water.** Aluminium species help destabilise fine suspended particles; flocs can trap particles and settle. Clarity is not proof of microbiological or chemical safety. [Water-treatment chemistry](https://guidelines.nhmrc.gov.au/australian-drinking-water-guidelines/part-5/treatment-chemicals/aluminium-sulfate-alum).

## Heat, air and light

25. **Wet clothes dry.** Water molecules escape from the surface into air. Moving air, temperature, exposed area and humidity affect evaporation. Evaporation occurs below boiling point too.
26. **Earthen pot cools water.** Water can seep through tiny pores and evaporate from the outside, carrying energy away. Cooling depends on humidity, airflow and the pot; the pot does not manufacture cold.
27. **Water drops outside a cold glass.** Water vapour already in air condenses on the cool surface when conditions permit. The drops do not usually pass through the glass.
28. **Metal spoon feels colder than wood.** At the same room temperature, metal generally conducts heat away from your hand faster. The sensation alone does not show metal has a lower temperature.
29. **A lid moves on a boiling vessel.** Expanding steam and pressure differences can exert force. Avoid using sealed containers or steam as a home experiment: burns and explosions are possible.
30. **Mirage on a hot road.** Light bends through air layers of different temperatures and refractive indices; the brain may interpret the light as coming from a reflection. It is not necessarily water on the road.
31. **Rainbow.** Light is refracted and dispersed as it enters raindrops, internally reflected, and refracted again as it leaves. Each colour exits at a different range of angles.
32. **Burning a candle.** Heat melts wax; liquid wax rises along the wick, vaporises, and wax vapour burns with oxygen. Complete and incomplete combustion can produce different products, including soot.
33. **Never “test” fire tricks at home.** An apparent fireproof hand or flame with hidden fuel does not prove immunity. Burns, toxic fumes and ignition of clothing are serious risks.
