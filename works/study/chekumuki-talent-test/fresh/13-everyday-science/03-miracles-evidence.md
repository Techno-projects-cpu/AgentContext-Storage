# Miracle or mechanism? Learn to investigate 🕵️

A surprising event is worth investigating, not blindly believing or automatically pretending to know the trick. **One observation can have several possible causes.** We describe mechanisms without chemical recipes; never use reagents on skin, food or eyes.

## Colours that can mislead

1. **“A lemon bleeds.”** Sufficiently acidic lemon juice can turn methyl orange red. Separately, Fe³⁺ and SCN⁻ can form a red complex. Hidden dye is another possible explanation. The colour alone does not prove which was used or that the liquid is blood. [Actual paper and two mechanisms](01-paper-questions.md).
2. **Turmeric turns reddish-brown with soap.** Curcumin acts as an indicator in sufficiently basic conditions. It is evidence consistent with an alkaline substance, not proof of a specific brand or exact pH.
3. **Red-cabbage or hibiscus indicator.** Pigments called anthocyanins can change colour with pH. The exact shade depends on the plant, concentration and solution; not all natural indicators behave the same.
4. **Phenolphthalein.** An acid–base indicator generally colourless in acidic/neutral conditions and pink in sufficiently alkaline conditions. It does not turn every liquid pink. Laboratory indicators must never be consumed.
5. **Starch and iodine.** Iodine solution can produce a blue-black colour with starch, associated with iodine/polyiodide interacting with starch's helical amylose. A colour test has limits and is not a full food-safety certification.
6. **“Milk has added urea.”** Your school paper Q3 mentions soybean powder, urease and red litmus: urease catalyses urea breakdown, releasing ammonia which can make the mixture basic, potentially turning red litmus blue. This is the **paper's intended idea**, not proof that any homemade test conclusively identifies adulteration; other substances can affect pH and food-testing labs use validated methods.
7. **Ferric thiocyanate red.** Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ can form a red complex. A red appearance by itself is not a blood test: several other mixtures can look red too.
8. **Alum and colloids.** Al³⁺ species can help destabilise particles. The wound question uses a colloid model, while water treatment uses flocculation; these situations must not be treated as identical.

## Separating evidence from performance

9. **Hidden variables:** an apparently plain container may have a residue, a sponge, a concealed compartment or a prepared surface. These are possible explanations, not a claim about any particular person without evidence.
10. **Control:** an untreated comparison can show whether a preparation matters. A control does not by itself eliminate every hidden variable.
11. **Change one factor:** if you change both the container and liquid, you cannot tell which difference caused the effect. A fair comparison holds other relevant factors as similar as feasible.
12. **Blind observation:** if the observer doesn't know which sample received a treatment, expectation is less likely to influence their judgement. It does not cure a badly designed study.
13. **Replication:** repeat with independent samples and record failures as well as successes. Repetition increases confidence but is not proof of every claimed explanation.
14. **Specificity:** if several chemicals produce a similar colour, colour alone isn't specific. Ask for additional independent tests from qualified professionals.
15. **Dose matters:** a substance can behave differently at different concentrations. “It is a chemical” does not automatically mean dangerous, and “natural” does not automatically mean safe.
16. **Acid vs indicator:** an acid changes the pH environment; an indicator is a substance whose colour depends on the environment. A colour does not mean acid turns into blood.
17. **Colloid vs clot:** a colloid contains fine dispersed particles; aggregation means particles come together. Biological haemostasis is a complex process; not every clump is a normal blood clot.
18. **Physical vs chemical:** ice melting rearranges water molecules without turning them into another substance; rusting creates new iron-containing substances. Observation alone may need supporting evidence to identify a reaction.
19. **A control question:** “If the effect depends on a coated surface, should a clean, otherwise similar surface behave differently?” The question is about design—not a recommendation to handle unknown reagents.
20. **Honesty about uncertainty:** if you cannot inspect the substances, say “consistent with …” rather than “this is definitely …”. A confident performer is not evidence of a supernatural claim or a specific chemical.

## 6-second connection quiz

- Lemon + methyl orange + low pH → **indicator changes form/colour**.
- Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ → **red iron–thiocyanate complex**.
- Alum + fine suspended particles → **destabilisation / flocculation** under suitable conditions.
- Starch + iodine → **blue-black complex**, with test limitations.
- Urea + urease → **ammonia**, potentially basic; validated testing requires more than a single colour.
- Soap + grease + water → **micelles and physical removal**.
